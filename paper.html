Who Made It, and Who Says It's Good? Dual Attribution in AI-Mediated Design Studios

Anonymous Author(s) · Submitted to CHI '28 · Alt. titles: “Two Empty Seats: Generative AI and the Authority Structure of the Design Studio” / “Dual Attribution: When AI Both Makes and Judges Student Design Work”

Abstract

Design studios rest on two forms of authority: authorial authority, an identifiable author of the work, and critical authority, an accountable owner of the standard by which the work is judged. Generative AI now occupies both seats at once. It produces parts of student work, and increasingly it also critiques that work. We propose dual attribution to describe how these two authorities become illegible together, and we argue that the two losses are coupled: a sense of authorship depends on owning the standard that makes one's contribution count. We report a formative study ([n] students, [n] instructors) and a within-subjects field experiment in which 16 four-person student teams completed four design sprints in Morrow, a team workspace that let us vary (a) whether AI-agent invocations were visible to teammates and (b) whether mid-sprint critique came from an instructor or an AI critic. Private invocation increased misattribution of contribution by [X]. AI critique shifted the vocabulary students used to justify design judgments toward the critic's by [Y]. When both were present, psychological ownership and students' ability to defend their designs dropped by [Z] beyond the sum of the two effects. We argue that disclosure labels and AI-use statements address the wrong problem, because what has been redistributed is authority, not information. We characterize design principles for authorizing mechanisms: ways of making someone answerable for a standard in AI-mediated creative education.

1 Introduction

In the final critique of a second-year service design course, an instructor points at a journey map and asks a team two ordinary questions: who made this part, and why is it good? Neither question gets an answer. The map was drafted by an AI agent that one teammate invoked in a private window, edited by a second teammate, and approved because an AI critic had called it “clear and user-centered” the night before. No one on the team is lying. The record simply does not contain the information the questions presuppose. [Replace with a real, anonymized vignette from Study 1.]

The design studio is often described by its furniture: desk crits, pin-ups, juries. We argue it is better understood as an authority structure. Two pillars hold it up. The first is authorial authority: a piece of work has an identifiable author who can be credited, questioned, and taught. The second is critical authority: the standard by which the work is judged has an owner, usually the instructor, who can be argued with and who answers for that standard [Schön 1987; Oh et al. 2013]. Students learn design by moving between the two positions. They make something, have it judged against a standard someone stands behind, and gradually take that standard on as their own.

Generative AI takes both seats at once. It produces drafts, images, and copy that enter student work, and multimodal models now also deliver critique on demand [Li et al. 2025; Schmitt-Fumian et al. 2026]. A growing body of HCI work studies the first seat: how AI assistance changes psychological ownership and credit in writing [Draxler et al. 2024; He et al. 2025; Hwang et al. 2025]. Separately, a long line of work studies critique and how to scale it [Luther et al. 2015; Yuan et al. 2016; Krause et al. 2017]. Qualitative studies of studio education report that GenAI “disrupts dialogic critique, undermines creative agency, diffuses ethical responsibility, and renders learning processes opaque” [Park 2025]. Yet these accounts treat the two seats separately. No study has asked whether the two losses interact.

We argue that they do, and that the interaction is the core problem. A sense of authorship is not only a feeling that “I made this”. It is the feeling that what I made counts, and judging whether it counts requires knowing what the work should be. If the standard has no accountable owner, a student cannot say what their contribution was for. We call the joint illegibility of who made the work and whose standard judges it dual attribution, and we predict that its two components combine superadditively.

We test this prediction in a real course using Morrow, a multimodal team workspace for design students that we built and deployed. Morrow serves here as an apparatus rather than as the contribution. It lets us vary, within the same teams, (a) whether agent invocations are visible on the shared timeline and (b) whether mid-sprint critique comes from the instructor or from an AI critic. We ask:

  • RQ1 When agent invocation is private, how far do teammates' and instructors' attributions of contribution diverge from logged contribution?
  • RQ2 When critique comes from an AI critic, whose standard do students use when they justify design judgments?
  • RQ3 Do private invocation and AI critique combine superadditively to reduce psychological ownership and the ability to defend one's design?

This paper makes three contributions. (1) A conceptual contribution: dual attribution, which recasts AI in studio education as a redistribution of authority rather than a disclosure problem. (2) An empirical contribution: a formative study and a 16-team within-subjects field experiment, including a new measure of standard internalization based on whose vocabulary students use to justify their judgments. (3) Design principles for authorizing mechanisms, which make a person answerable for AI-produced work and AI-voiced standards, together with a critique of disclosure-based fixes.

2 Related Work

2.1 Authorship and ownership in AI-assisted creation

Work on AI-assisted writing consistently finds a gap between felt and declared authorship. Draxler et al. describe an “AI ghostwriter effect”: people feel less ownership of AI-assisted text yet still decline to credit the AI publicly [Draxler et al. 2024]. He, Houde, and Weisz show that equal contributions earn AI less credit than humans [He et al. 2025]. Hwang et al. find that writers locate authenticity in the process rather than in the final product [Hwang et al. 2025], and Kobiella et al. document professionals' ambivalence when the machine performs “as good as me” [Kobiella et al. 2024]. A natural remedy is to make provenance visible. HaLLMark shows that visualizing AI contributions helps writers preserve ownership [Hoque et al. 2024]. These studies ground ownership in theories of psychological ownership, which hold that ownership develops through control, intimate knowledge, and self-investment [Pierce et al. 2001], and recent CHI work finds that ownership's positive effect weakens when AI leads the collaboration [Hong and Lee 2026]. Two features limit this literature for our purposes. Almost all of it studies individuals, and it holds the evaluator constant. Who judges the work is never a variable.

2.2 Critique and the ownership of standards

Critique is the studio's central pedagogical instrument. Schön describes the studio as a reflective practicum in which students learn to see as the master sees [Schön 1983; 1987], and Oh et al. formalize the moves of architectural critique [Oh et al. 2013]. HCI has largely treated critique as a scarce resource to be scaled. CrowdCrit structures crowd feedback [Luther et al. 2015], Yuan et al. show that novices can approach expert feedback with scaffolding [Yuan et al. 2016], Krause et al. propose a critique style guide [Krause et al. 2017], and Kulkarni et al. calibrate peer assessment at scale [Kulkarni et al. 2013]. Each of these systems assumes that the standard has an owner, such as a rubric author, an instructor, or a calibrated crowd. Recent work on LLM-as-a-judge shows that this assumption becomes fragile once evaluation criteria are written by one person and applied by a model [Chiang et al. 2026], and that co-writing with an opinionated model can shift users' views without their awareness [Jakesch et al. 2023]. Fu et al. find that GenAI-supported designs were rated more novel, but clients saw no gain in brand alignment [Fu et al. 2026]. Whether a design is “good” depends on whose standard is applied.

2.3 Teams, identifiability, and AI teammates

Social psychology has long shown that unidentifiable contributions change group behavior. Social loafing grows when individual input cannot be identified [Latané et al. 1979], and the Collective Effort Model ties effort to the expectation that one's contribution will be evaluated [Karau and Williams 1993]. AI makes contributions harder to identify, and in an unusual direction. Students may not be loafing at all, but the record no longer shows what they did. Studies of teams working with GenAI find that human partners reduce reliance on AI during collaborative prompting [Han et al. 2024], that shared displays can support mutual awareness in AI-assisted brainstorming [Zhang et al. 2025], that small creative teams negotiate AI as a “potential teammate” [Panchanadikar and Freeman 2026], and that people accept AI monitoring as support only under specific conditions [Weng et al. 2026]. Identity also matters independently of behavior: identical drawing moves receive lower creativity ratings when attributed to an AI partner [Won et al. 2026]. In parallel, GenAI has been linked to fixation and homogenization [Wadinambiarachchi et al. 2024; Doshi and Hauser 2024; Anderson et al. 2024], which Dalsgaard theorizes as “combinatorial capture” [Dalsgaard 2026].

2.4 Generative AI in studio-based design education

Design education research has begun to document GenAI in the studio. Surveys show that students use AI selectively, more for concept development and written articulation than for visual refinement [Huh et al. 2026]. Instructors evaluating an MLLM critique prototype expressed both expectations and reservations [Li et al. 2025], and students and professors imagine AI feedback as shared support under time pressure [Schmitt-Fumian et al. 2026]. A systematic review maps GenAI-enhanced conceptual design [Fang et al. 2025]. The closest work to ours, Park's qualitative study, concludes that GenAI disrupts dialogic critique and creative agency and recommends that studio pedagogy emphasize process transparency [Park 2025]. HCI systems have begun to cast AI as an antagonistic critic for novice designers [Han and Martelaro 2026] and to delegate critique while “preserving human decision authority” [Okazaki et al. 2026].

Gap. These literatures each address one pillar. Authorship research varies who makes the work and holds the judge constant. Critique research varies the judge and assumes the author is known. Design education research observes both pillars weakening but describes the phenomenon without testing a mechanism. Its main remedy, transparency, addresses only the first pillar. We test whether the two pillars depend on each other.

4 Apparatus: Morrow

Morrow is a multimodal team workspace for design students that combines a shared canvas, a per-member AI agent, a version timeline, and a critique panel (Fig. 2). We describe only the two parameters manipulated in this study. Other features were held constant across conditions.

Invocation visibility. In the visible setting, every agent invocation is posted to the team timeline. The post shows the invoker, the prompt, the output, and which elements of the output were placed on the canvas. Canvas elements carry provenance (for example “manual”, “AI-generated”, “edited AI”). In the private setting, the agent lives in a personal side panel, and output copied to the canvas carries no provenance. This mirrors common practice, in which students use a separate chat window. We chose not to show provenance as a percentage, because a percentage implies a precision about contribution that the logs cannot support.

Critique source. In the instructor setting, the mid-sprint critique is a 15-minute instructor session, with written notes posted to the critique panel. In the AI setting, the critique panel is filled by an AI critic, a multimodal model with a fixed prompt that produces structured comments (strengths, concerns, suggested revisions) in a format and length matched to the instructor's notes. The final evaluation was always done by the instructor using a fixed rubric, so that grading was fair across conditions. Final grades were not a dependent variable.

Logging. Morrow logs invoker_id, prompt, output_id, ai_granularity, parent_version_id, action (accept / modify / discard), visibility_setting, and critique_source. Logged contribution share for each member is computed as [define: e.g., weighted share of canvas elements in the final version authored or substantively modified by the member, with AI-generated elements attributed to the invoker only if modified].

5 Study 2: Method

5.1 Participants and setting

We recruited 64 students (16 teams of four) from [course] at [institution], a design course in [semester 2027]. Participation in the research was voluntary and separate from course credit. Students who declined used the same course materials without data collection. The study was approved by [IRB] and pre-registered at [OSF link].

5.2 Design

We used a 2 (invocation visibility: visible / private) × 2 (critique source: instructor / AI) within-team design. Each team completed four one-week design sprints, one per condition, on four briefs matched for scope and difficulty [list briefs]. Condition order and condition–brief pairing were counterbalanced with a 4 × 4 Latin square, giving four groups of four teams. Within a sprint, the team met twice in class and worked asynchronously between meetings.

5.3 Procedure

Each sprint followed the same sequence: brief release (day 1) → team work in Morrow → mid-sprint critique from the condition's source (day 3–4) → revision → submission (day 7) → post-sprint measures (≈25 min) → instructor final evaluation. After the fourth sprint, we interviewed 20 students and all instructors. Students in AI-critique sprints received the instructor's written feedback after the study, so that no one lost instructional feedback.

We fit linear mixed models, DV ~ visibility × source + sprint_position + brief + (1 | team/student), using lme4, and binomial GLMMs for proportions. We report effect sizes with 95% CIs and test the interaction on the original scale, which is the scale at which superadditivity is defined. Sample size was set by simulation-based power analysis (simr) using effect sizes from a two-team pilot [report pilot]. Interview data were analyzed with reflexive thematic analysis, with particular attention to cases that contradicted the quantitative pattern.

7 Discussion

7.1 Why the losses compound

Our results suggest that authorship in the studio is not a private feeling about provenance. It is a claim that depends on a standard. A student can say “I made this part” in a way that matters only if someone can say what the part was supposed to achieve. When the critic is an AI, students still hear a standard, and its vocabulary enters their judgments, as our internalization measure shows. But no one stands behind that standard. Nobody can be argued with and nobody answers for it. When invocation is also private, there is no record to anchor the authorial claim either. The student is left with a contribution no one can see, measured against a standard no one owns. This explains why the two losses combine rather than add. Each pillar is what would let a student recover from the loss of the other.

7.2 Why disclosure is a partial fix

The dominant institutional response to AI in creative education is disclosure: AI-use statements, provenance labels, and contribution percentages. Provenance visualization does help with the first pillar [Hoque et al. 2024], and our visible condition reproduces that benefit. Disclosure, however, redistributes information, while the studio's problem is the redistribution of authority. A label that reads “this critique was generated by AI” tells a student where a standard came from. It does not give them anyone to contest it with or anyone who will answer for it. The same applies to Park's call for process transparency [Park 2025]. Transparency is necessary, but our results suggest it is not sufficient.

7.3 Designing authorizing mechanisms

We use authorizing mechanisms for interaction designs that make a person answerable for AI-produced content or AI-voiced standards. We derive four principles from our findings: (P1) Claimed critique. AI critique becomes visible to the team only after an instructor or a designated student endorses, edits, or rejects each point, so that the standard gets an owner. (P2) Invocation as a team act. Agent outputs placed on shared work carry the invoker's name, and in a later round that person explains them, which turns provenance into accountability. (P3) Standard-before-score. Following the move by Okazaki et al. to present criteria and uncertainties rather than verdicts [Okazaki et al. 2026], AI critics should state which standard they apply and invite contestation. (P4) Defense as evidence. Assessment should weight a student's ability to defend decisions alongside the artifact, because the artifact alone no longer shows who did what.

7.4 Implications for assessment in creative education

If portfolios can no longer show who did what or whose standard they meet, assessment that relies on artifacts loses validity as evidence of learning. This is a fairness problem as much as a pedagogical one, since a single student working with an agent can produce a team-sized portfolio. Our results point toward assessing the process and its defense, and toward making the studio's public object the process itself rather than only the finished artifact.

7.5 If the interaction is null (alternate framing)

[Use if H3 is not supported.] Visibility and critique source each had independent effects but did not interact. This suggests the two authorities are separable: institutions can address authorship (through provenance) and the ownership of standards (through claimed critique) as distinct design problems, and fixing one will neither substitute for nor amplify the other. This finding is also informative, because it rules out the common hope that provenance labels alone will resolve students' sense of authorship in AI-mediated critique.

8 Limitations and Future Work

Our study took place in a single institution and course, with week-long sprints. Our claim is about a mechanism, not the size of an effect in a population. We used a single configuration of the AI critic, and a critic with a stated persona or a different format might lead students to internalize its standard differently. Logged contribution is itself a construct: our share metric privileges canvas elements over discussion, which logs capture poorly. Most importantly, our AI condition confounds two things: the presence of an AI critic and its unaccountability. A critic whose points are claimed by an instructor (P1) would separate them. We see that comparison as the next question: whether students lose critical authority because the critic is a machine or because no one answers for what it says.

9 Conclusion

We return to the final critique: who made this part, and why is it good? The two questions turn out to be one. Students can answer the first only if someone answers for the second. As generative AI takes both the author's seat and the critic's, the work of HCI is not only to label what the machine did but to design ways for a person to stand behind it again.
